# How to use AI Agents, from the basics to Pro

Script draft for task #614. Lines in [brackets] are for the edit or for you to fill in, and everything else is spoken.

## 1. Opening

Most people use an AI agent the way they use a search box. They type a question, read the answer, and copy the useful part somewhere else.

That works, but you're still doing the job. You've only got faster help with one step of it.

In this video I'll take you from that starting point to running agents as part of how you work. There are five levels. Each one needs one new habit, and none of them need you to write code.

[ON SCREEN: the five levels as a list: Delegate, Brief, Equip, Verify, Systemise]

## 2. What an agent actually is

First, the difference between a chatbot and an agent.

A chatbot answers. An agent acts. You give it a goal, and it works in a loop: it decides on a step, uses a tool, looks at what happened, and decides on the next step. It keeps going until the job is done or it gets stuck.

The tool might be your files, a browser, a database or your calendar. That's the part that matters, because an agent can change things in the world. It can also be wrong while it's doing that.

So the question changes. With a chatbot you ask, "What can I ask it?" With an agent you ask, "What can I hand over, and how will I know it went well?"

The rest of this video is about answering that.

## 3. Level one: Delegate

Level one is giving it a whole task instead of a question.

Instead of "What should a good project update include?", try "Read these notes and write this week's project update for my team."

The first request gets you advice. The second gets you a draft you can use.

Pick something small and low-risk for your first try. Something you could do yourself in half an hour, and could check in two minutes. Summarising a folder of notes is a good start. So is cleaning up a messy spreadsheet copy.

[DEMO 1: hand an agent one small, real task from your own week and let it run. Show the steps it takes, not just the result.]

Watch what it does while it works. You'll learn more from the steps than from the final answer, because you'll see where it guesses.

## 4. Level two: Brief

When people say an agent gave them a bad result, the cause is usually a bad brief.

Think of it as a capable new colleague on their first day. They're smart, but they know nothing about your work. So a good brief has four parts.

The goal: what you want, and why you want it.

The context: the files, the background, who this is for.

The limits: what it shouldn't touch, and what to do if it's unsure.

And the most important one, what done looks like. "Write a report" has no finish line. "A one-page report, with three recommendations, each one backed by a number from this file" does.

When you state what done looks like, the agent can check its own work against it, and so can you.

[ON SCREEN: Goal, Context, Limits, Done]

Then, if you find yourself correcting the same thing twice, write it down. Most agent tools have somewhere to keep standing instructions. Your preferences stop being something you repeat and become something it already knows.

## 5. Level three: Equip

At level three you decide what the agent can reach.

An agent with no tools can only talk. Connect it to your documents, your task list or your data, and it can do real work on your real material.

This is also where it gets risky, so here's the rule I'd follow. Let it read widely, and let it write narrowly.

Reading is usually safe. Writing, sending, deleting and paying are not, because they're hard to take back. Start with read-only access. Then, for anything that changes something, have it show you what it's about to do and wait for your yes.

[DEMO 2: an agent reads from a real data source, proposes a change, and stops to ask for confirmation before writing.]

One more thing to watch. Anything an agent reads could contain instructions, like a web page or an email someone sent you. Treat that content as information rather than orders, and be most careful when an agent can both read outside material and take actions.

## 6. Level four: Verify

Here's the trap. Agents sound confident whether they're right or wrong, and "I've finished" isn't evidence that it's finished.

So at level four you stop asking "Is it done?" and start asking "Show me."

If it wrote code, did the tests run, and what did they say? If it changed a record, read it back. If it quotes a number, where did that number come from?

You can build this into the brief. Ask it to check its work against your definition of done and report anything it couldn't confirm. A good report says what's proven and what's still a guess.

[DEMO 3: ask an agent to check its own finished work against the brief. Ideally show it catching a real mistake.]

Also, match your checking to the stakes. A draft you'll read anyway needs a quick look. Anything that goes out under your name, or moves money, or touches customers gets a real review. You can hand over the work, but you still own the result.

## 7. Level five: Systemise

Up to now, you've been running one task at a time. Level five is where it starts to feel different.

Three things change.

First, repeated work becomes reusable. When you've got a task that works well, save the brief, the steps and the checks, so that next time you just say "run the weekly report".

Second, you split work into specialists. One agent researches, another drafts, and another reviews the draft. A reviewer that didn't write the work tends to be a tougher judge of it.

Third, your job changes. You spend less time doing the work and more time deciding what's worth doing, writing clear briefs, and reviewing results. That means keeping a real list of what's been handed over, what came back, and what passed review.

[YOUR EXAMPLE: thirty seconds on how you run this yourself. Show your own task list and an agent's finished work waiting for your review.]

The pros aren't the people with the cleverest prompts. They're the people with the best systems around the agent.

## 8. Close

So here's the path again.

Delegate a whole task. Brief it properly. Equip it carefully. Verify what comes back. Then systemise what works.

You don't need to do all of this at once. This week, pick one small task you'd normally do yourself, write a four-part brief, and hand it over. Then check the result and notice where the brief let it down.

That's the whole practice, and you get better at it by repeating it.
